What this guide is, and who it is for

A live stream is three to ten hours of mostly nothing, with a handful of moments that are genuinely worth sharing. Clipping is the craft of finding those moments fast and packaging each one so a stranger scrolling a phone stops and watches.

This guide is for you if

  • you clip a stream you own, or one whose streamer has said yes
  • you want a repeatable process, not luck
  • you may want to automate part of it

You will learn

  • how clipping works end to end
  • how to pick moments
  • the 4 signals that point to them
  • layout and caption rules for vertical video
  • permission and copyright basics
  • a 7-day starter plan

Chapter 1

How stream clipping works

Every clipping workflow, manual or automated, is the same five steps. The skill is in steps 2 and 3.

1

Capture

Record the stream and its chat with a timestamp. You need the video, the audio and the chat log, all on the same clock. Without chat you lose your best signal.

2

Detect

Look for moments where something changed: chat speeds up, the streamer gets louder, the words get urgent. These are candidates, not clips yet.

3

Judge

Decide whether a candidate is a standalone story. Is there a hook, a payoff and enough context for a stranger? This is where most clips die, and they should.

4

Cut

Trim to the story (20 to 55 seconds works well), reframe for a vertical screen, add captions and a hook title, normalise the sound, export a cover image.

5

Review and publish

A human watches every clip before it goes out. Check the first frame, the captions, the music, and that the streamer is credited.

Speed matters

Moments are worth more the sooner they are posted. In our test replay, the automated path took about 49 seconds on average from the moment happening to a finished clip. By hand, budget 10 to 20 minutes per clip.

Chapter 2

Picking moments worth a clip

A good clip is a tiny story: a hook in the first two seconds, a build, a payoff, and a clean ending. If a viewer needs the other three hours to understand it, it is not a clip.

1

The hook

Something happens or is said in the first two seconds that makes a stranger stay: a shout, a shocked face, a bold line, a sudden fail. Start right before it, not 20 seconds before.

2

The payoff

The reaction, the punchline, the clutch landing, the twist. End shortly after it. Never cut mid-sentence.

Moment types that tend to work

Type Why it works
Big reaction Shock, rage, laughter, panic. Faces and voices carry emotion without context.
Fail or comeback A clear before and after inside 30 seconds.
Chat interaction The streamer reads or reacts to chat. Viewers feel part of it.
Roast or hot take A short, quotable opinion with a clear target.
Clutch or skill A visible win against the odds. Needs the screen, not just the face.
Punchline story A short anecdote that lands. Check the audio is clear.

Quick filter before you cut

Cut it when
  • it makes sense with zero context
  • the first 2 seconds are interesting
  • there is a visible or audible payoff
  • it fits in 20 to 55 seconds
Skip it when
  • it is menus, loading or silence
  • the funny part needs an in-joke
  • loud copyrighted music is underneath
  • the content is unsafe for the platform
Rule of thumb. Five strong clips beat twenty weak ones. In our test of 14 automatic clips from one stream, about 4 were good, 5 fine and 5 weak, so a human review step is not optional.

Chapter 3

The 4 signals that point to a moment

You do not have to watch every minute. Four signals, checked together, tell you where to look.

Signal 1

Chat velocity

Messages per second against the last 10 minutes of chat. A sudden jump means viewers just saw something. Chat reacts about 10 to 15 seconds late, so look back from the peak.

Signal 2

Hype words

Laughing emojis, "lol", "W", "L", "no way", channel emotes, superchats. Spam of the same short word is a stronger sign than long messages.

Signal 3

Audio energy

The loudest quarter second of each second, compared with the streamer's own baseline. Shouting, laughing and crashes stand out even when the words are unclear.

Signal 4

Speech

A transcript with word timings shows what was said, powers captions, and lets you cut exactly at a sentence boundary.

How to combine them

  • Compare to a rolling baseline, not a fixed number. A busy chat at the start of a stream is not a spike. A jump above the last 10 minutes is.
  • Reward agreement. Chat and audio rising together is worth far more than either alone.
  • Merge peaks that are close. Two spikes under 45 seconds apart are usually one moment.
  • Sample quiet stretches too. If nothing fires for five minutes, still look at the best window in that span.
  • The signals find candidates; a judge decides. The final call is a human (or an AI judge that also sees the screen) asking: is this a story?

Chapter 4

Vertical layout rules

Shorts, Reels and TikTok are 1080 x 1920 pixels (9:16). A 16:9 stream shrinks to a thin strip if you only scale it, so reframe it.

Platform header
Facecam
zoomed
Gameplay /
screen
CAPTION WORDS
Platform UI:
caption, buttons
keep clear

Stacked layout, proportions approximate

The rules

  • Face on top, action below. Crop and zoom the webcam to fill the top 35 to 40 percent. Put the gameplay or screen below it. A thin divider line helps the eye.
  • Keep the important part out of the danger zones. The top ~8 percent and the bottom ~20 to 25 percent are covered by platform buttons and descriptions. Right edge too. These zones change, so test on a real phone.
  • Captions in the middle band, between the two panels or just under the action, never in the bottom strip.
  • Hook title for the first 2 to 3 seconds. Three lines max, big, high contrast, gone before the payoff.
  • First frame is the cover. On YouTube Shorts frame zero often is the thumbnail, so make it readable.
  • Length 20 to 55 seconds is a good range; shorter if the story is short.
  • Sound at about -14 LUFS. Normalise it so clips do not jump in volume.
  • Credit the streamer on screen and in the caption.
No facecam? Use a centred crop of the action with a blurred, scaled copy of the frame behind it. Never leave black bars.

Chapter 5

Caption rules

Most people watch with the sound low or off. Captions are not decoration, they are how the clip is understood. There are two kinds: burned-in words on the video, and the post caption underneath.

On-video captions

  • 2 to 3 words at a time. Short chunks match speech and are easy to read.
  • Highlight the active word (a colour change) so the eye follows the voice.
  • Time to the word, not the sentence. Use word timestamps from speech-to-text.
  • No overlapping chunks. One caption on screen at a time.
  • Big, bold, high contrast, with a dark outline or shadow.
  • Middle band only (see layout rules).

Post captions

  • First line is the hook. Many platforms cut after one or two lines.
  • One sentence of context, then the credit.
  • 3 to 5 relevant hashtags, not 30.
  • Credit the streamer every time.
  • One call to action at most: watch the full stream, or follow.

Fix speech-to-text mistakes

Automatic transcripts of a loud game mix are never perfect. Names, slang and game terms are the usual errors. Always:

  • read the captions of every clip before it goes out,
  • keep a small list of the streamer's names and catchphrases and correct them,
  • star or soften strong language if the platform limits it,
  • set the transcription language to the stream's language.

Hook title examples

THE PLAN
NOBODY
SAW COMING
RAGE QUIT,
THEN INSTANT
COMEBACK
CHAT CALLED
THE ENDING

Six words or fewer. Say what happens, create a question, never lie about the content.

Chapter 6

Permission and copyright basics

This is the most important page. Clipping is easy. Clipping safely means you have the right to do it.

The rule: only clip streams you have permission to clip. Credit does not replace permission. A streamer owns their stream, and the platform and game publisher may have rights too.

What to do

1

Ask, in writing. A short message is enough: you will clip their streams, credit them on every post, and remove anything they ask you to remove. Keep the reply.

2

Agree the terms. Which platforms, whether you may monetise, whether they want to approve clips first, and how to take a clip down.

3

Credit every time. Name or handle on screen and in the caption, plus a link to the full stream.

4

Check the music. Streams often play commercial songs. Even with the streamer's yes, the music label can claim or mute your clip. If a clip has a track under it, skip it or strip the music.

5

Respect platform rules. Each platform has its own policy on reused content, sexual content, violence and minors. Review every clip.

Quick risk table

Situation Risk Action
Your own stream Low Check music and platform rules
Streamer said yes in writing Low to medium Credit, keep proof, check music
No contact, credit only High Do not post. Ask first
Clip contains a commercial song High Skip, or remove the music
Sexual or graphic scene High Reject the clip

This page is general information, not legal advice. Rules differ by country and platform. When in doubt, ask the rights holder.

Your plan

The 7-day starter plan

Aim: permission secured, a repeatable routine, and 5 to 7 reviewed clips published by day 7. About 1 to 2 hours a day.

DAY 1PERMISSION

Pick one streamer and get a yes. Choose a stream you like and understand. Send the permission message from page 8. Pick the platform you will post on first (one is enough).

DAY 2TOOLS

Set up your tools. Install ffmpeg and yt-dlp, make a folder structure (captures, clips, finished), and record or download one past stream plus its chat.

DAY 3FIND

Find 10 candidates. Scrub the chat for bursts and the audio for loud peaks. Write down the timestamps. Do not cut yet.

DAY 4JUDGE

Cut the best 5 on paper. For each, find the hook second and the payoff second. Drop any that need context or have music underneath. Keep the 5 strongest.

DAY 5CUT

Make your first 5 vertical clips. Stacked layout, word-timed captions, hook title, normalised sound, cover frame. Follow pages 6 and 7.

DAY 6REVIEW

Review like a stranger. Watch each on a phone with sound off, then on. Check first frame, captions, credit, music. Write each post caption and 3 to 5 hashtags.

DAY 7PUBLISH

Publish and learn. Post 1 to 2 clips, spaced a few hours apart. Check views after 24 hours: which hook, length and moment type did best? Use that for week 2.

Then automate. Once you can do this by hand, the open-source Live Clipper (next page) automates days 3 to 5 and leaves the review and permission steps to you, where they belong.
